Performance: CPI, IPC & Amdahl's Law

The only honest measure of speed is how long a real program takes. That time splits into three factors: how many instructions × how many clock cycles each × how long one cycle is. Amdahl's Law then tells you how much a speed-up to one part can help the whole.

Think of it like this: a delivery route. Total time = number of stops × minutes spent at each stop × how fast you drive. Buying a faster van only speeds up the driving. If most of the day is spent at the stops, a faster van barely helps. That is Amdahl's Law.

Key terms

Execution (CPU) time
Seconds the CPU spends running the program. Lower is better.
Performance
1 / execution time. Higher is better.
Speedup
old time / new time. "2× faster" means the new time is half the old.
IC (instruction count)
How many instructions the program runs.
CPI
Clock cycles per instruction, on average. Single-cycle = 1. A pipeline with stalls is a little above 1.
Clock cycle time (T)
Length of one clock tick. Clock rate = 1 / T. For example, 250 ps ↔ 4 GHz.
IPC
Instructions per cycle = 1 / CPI. Higher is better. Superscalar CPUs reach IPC > 1.
MIPS (the metric)
Million Instructions Per Second. Not the same thing as the MIPS processor! Often misleading.
Units cheat-sheet

1 ps = 10−12 s · 1 ns = 10−9 s · 1 GHz = 109 cycles per second. So a 250 ps cycle means 1 / (250 × 10−12) = 4 × 109 Hz = 4 GHz.

1. Performance and speedup

Performance = 1 / Execution timeA faster computer takes less time.
Speedup of X over Y = TimeY / TimeX = PerformanceX / PerformanceY"X is n times faster than Y" means n = TimeY / TimeX.

Example: a program takes 10 s on A and 15 s on B. A is 15 / 10 = 1.5× faster than B.

2. The CPU performance equation (the "iron law")

CPU time = IC × CPI × T = (IC × CPI) / clock rateseconds/program = instructions/program × cycles/instruction × seconds/cycle. The units cancel like fractions.

Worked example (P&H): same ISA, two computers

Given: computer A has cycle time 250 ps and CPI 2.0. Computer B has cycle time 500 ps and CPI 1.2. Same program, so same IC. Which is faster, and by how much?

Time per instruction: A = 2.0 × 250 = 500 ps. B = 1.2 × 500 = 600 ps.

Whole program: A = IC × 500 ps, B = IC × 600 ps. IC cancels out when we divide.

Speedup: TimeB / TimeA = 600 / 500 = 1.2. A is 1.2× faster, even though its CPI is worse.

Worked example (P&H): designing a faster clock

  1. A program runs in 10 s on computer A, which has a 2 GHz clock. So A uses 10 s × 2 × 109 = 20 × 109 cycles.
  2. We want computer B to take 6 s. But B's design makes the program need 1.2× as many cycles: 1.2 × 20 × 109 = 24 × 109 cycles.
  3. Clock rateB = cycles / time = 24 × 109 / 6 s = 4 × 109 Hz = 4 GHz.

Modern chip makers often quote an "IPC uplift", e.g. "+15% IPC": the new chip does 15% more work per cycle at the same clock rate.

5. The MIPS metric, and why it misleads

MIPS = IC / (Execution time × 106) = Clock rate / (CPI × 106)For example, a 4 GHz clock with CPI 2 gives 4 × 109 / (2 × 106) = 2000 MIPS.

Classes A, B, C have CPI 1, 2 and 3, and the clock is 4 GHz. Two compilers produce (in billions of instructions):

MIPS rewards doing more, simpler work

Compiler 2 has the higher MIPS but is slower. MIPS also can't compare different ISAs (one x86 instruction may do the work of several MIPS instructions), and it changes from program to program. Only execution time is reliable.

6. Amdahl's Law

You speed up part of a program. The part you didn't touch still takes the same time, so it limits the total gain.

Speedupoverall = 1 / ( (1 − f) + f / s )f = fraction of the original time that is improved · s = how many times faster that part becomes.
Limit as s → ∞: Speedupmax = 1 / (1 − f)Even an infinitely fast improvement can't remove the untouched part.
  1. Old time = 1Split it into (1 − f), the untouched part, and f, the improved part.
  2. New time(1 − f) stays the same. f shrinks to f / s.
  3. Speedupold / new = 1 / ((1 − f) + f / s).

Worked example (P&H): speeding up multiply

A program runs for 100 s. Multiplication takes 80 s of that (f = 0.8). Drawn to scale:

  1. Goal 4×: new time = 100 / 4 = 25 s. Multiply must take 25 − 20 = 5 s, so it must be 80 / 5 = 16× faster.
  2. Goal 5×: new time = 20 s, which leaves 0 s for multiply. Impossible.
  3. Check with the formula: f = 0.8, s = 4 gives 1 / (0.2 + 0.8 / 4) = 1 / (0.2 + 0.2) = 2.5× ✓.

Takeaway: make the common case fast

Spend your effort on the part that takes the most time. Improving a small part a lot gives less than improving a big part a little. See Power wall & multicore for why this matters for today's many-core chips.

Common mistakes & exam traps

Slips that cost marks
  • Speedup = old / new, not new / old. The answer should be greater than 1 if things got faster.
  • Don't compare computers using CPI alone or clock rate alone. Multiply all three factors.
  • In Amdahl's Law, f is a fraction of time, not of code lines or of instructions.
  • With s → ∞ the answer is 1 / (1 − f), not ∞.
  • Watch the units: convert ps/ns and GHz before multiplying.
  • The MIPS metric (millions of instructions per second) is not the MIPS processor, and higher MIPS doesn't mean faster.
  • Weighted CPI uses the share of instructions in each class, not the share of time.

Practice

A program has IC = 2 × 109, CPI = 1.5 and a 2.5 GHz clock. What is the CPU time?

Time = IC × CPI / rate = 2 × 109 × 1.5 / (2.5 × 109) = 1.2 s.

A new compiler lowers CPI to 1.2, but its instructions need a 10% longer clock period. IC stays the same. Is it faster, and by how much?

New T = 1.1 / 2.5 GHz = 0.44 ns. New time = 2 × 109 × 1.2 × 0.44 × 10−9 = 1.056 s. Speedup = 1.2 / 1.056 ≈ 1.14×, so yes, it is faster.

What is the MIPS rating of a 4 GHz CPU with average CPI 2?

4 × 109 / (2 × 106) = 2000 MIPS.

Floating-point work takes 40% of a program's time. It is made 10× faster. What is the overall speedup? What is the most possible?

1 / (0.6 + 0.4 / 10) = 1 / 0.64 ≈ 1.56×. Maximum = 1 / 0.6 ≈ 1.67×.

Option 1: make 60% of the time 2× faster. Option 2: make 30% of the time 10× faster. Which is better?

Option 1: 1 / (0.4 + 0.3) = 1 / 0.7 ≈ 1.43×. Option 2: 1 / (0.7 + 0.03) = 1 / 0.73 ≈ 1.37×. Option 1 wins, because it speeds up the common case.

A program is 90% parallel. How many cores are needed for a 4× speedup?

4 = 1 / (0.1 + 0.9 / s) → 0.1 + 0.9 / s = 0.25 → 0.9 / s = 0.15 → s = 6 cores.

Instruction classes: 50% with CPI 1, 30% with CPI 2, 20% with CPI 3. IC = 106 and T = 1 ns. What are the CPU time and the IPC?

CPI = 0.5 + 0.6 + 0.6 = 1.7. Time = 106 × 1.7 × 10−9 = 1.7 ms. IPC = 1 / 1.7 ≈ 0.59.

Can you make a program 5× faster by improving a part that takes 80% of its time?

No. The best possible is 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 5×, and that needs the improved part to take zero time. Any real improvement gives less than 5×.
