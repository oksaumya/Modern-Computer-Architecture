The power wall & why every chip went multicore

For about 30 years, CPUs got faster mainly by ticking their clock faster. Around 2005 that stopped: a faster clock made the chip too hot to cool. Chip makers still got more transistors every generation, so they used them to build more cores instead of one faster core.

Think of it like this: one cook can only chop so fast before their hands overheat. Past that point, you don't train the cook to go faster. You hire a second cook. That only helps if the recipe can be split into jobs they can do at the same time.

Key terms

Dynamic power
Power used each time transistors switch between 0 and 1. It is most of a busy chip's power.
Clock frequency (f)
How many clock ticks happen per second, in hertz (Hz). 3 GHz = 3 billion ticks per second.
Supply voltage (V)
The voltage that powers the transistors. A lower V saves power but makes transistors slower.
Leakage current
Current that trickles through a transistor even when it is "off". It wastes power as heat.
Power density
Power per unit of chip area (W/cm²). Too high and the chip overheats.
Moore's law
The observation that the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles every two years.
Dennard scaling
The rule that smaller transistors could also use a lower voltage, so power density stayed the same.
Power wall
The limit on how much heat a chip can get rid of cheaply. It stopped clock speeds from rising.
Dark silicon
Parts of a chip that must stay switched off at any moment, because powering everything at once would overheat it.
Core
One complete processor (datapath + control) on a chip. A multicore chip has several.
SMP
Symmetric multiprocessing: all cores are the same and share one main memory.
Throughput
How much work gets finished per second (for example, tasks per second).

1. Where the power goes: P = α·C·V²·f

Every time a transistor switches, it charges or drains a tiny capacitor. That costs energy. Add it up over the whole chip and you get dynamic power:

P = α · C · V² · fα (alpha) = the fraction of transistors switching each tick · C = total capacitance being switched · V = supply voltage · f = clock frequency
  • f is in the formula directly: double the clock speed and you double the power.
  • V is squared, so voltage matters most. Cut V by 10% and power falls by about 19% (0.9² = 0.81).
  • A faster clock usually also needs a higher voltage, so raising f costs even more than the formula suggests.

Worked example: lower V and f by 15%

  1. New voltage V' = 0.85 V. New frequency f' = 0.85 f. α and C stay the same.
  2. P' = α · C · (0.85 V)² · (0.85 f) = 0.85² × 0.85 × (α·C·V²·f) = 0.85³ × P.
  3. 0.85³ = 0.85 × 0.85 × 0.85 = 0.614, so P' ≈ 0.61 P. That is about 39% less power for only 15% less speed.
  4. The multicore trick: use two such slowed-down cores. Power = 2 × 0.61 P ≈ 1.23 P. Work done can reach 2 × 0.85 = 1.7×, but only if the job splits perfectly in two.

2. Dennard scaling: the free lunch

In 1974 Robert Dennard noticed a happy rule. Each new process generation shrinks transistor sizes by about 0.7×. If you also lower the voltage by 0.7×, the numbers work out like this:

QuantityChange per generationWhy
Length, width× 0.7The transistor shrinks.
Area per transistor× 0.49 (0.7 × 0.7)So twice as many fit in the same space (Moore's law).
Capacitance C× 0.7A smaller transistor has less to charge.
Voltage V× 0.7Scaled down together with the size.
Frequency f× 1.4 (1 ÷ 0.7)Shorter distances switch faster.
Power per transistor0.7 × 0.7² × 1.4 ≈ × 0.49From P = α·C·V²·f.
Power density0.49 ÷ 0.49 = × 1 (unchanged)Power and area both shrink by the same amount.

So every generation brought more transistors and a faster clock at the same heat per cm². Software simply got faster by itself. People called this the "free lunch".

3. The breakdown (around 2005–2006)

  1. Voltage stuckBelow roughly 1 V, transistors cannot switch off cleanly.
  2. Leakage growsLowering the switching threshold makes "off" transistors leak much more current.
  3. So V stops fallingWithout a lower V, a faster f means more heat per cm².
  4. Power wallAbout 100 W/cm² is the practical limit for cheap air cooling.
  5. Clock stallsDesktop CPUs stay around 3–4 GHz from then on.
Moore's law did not end at the same time

Transistor counts kept rising. What ended was Dennard scaling: the ability to use those transistors at a higher speed without more heat. The extra transistors went into more cores, bigger caches and special accelerators. Because not all of them can be powered at once, some sit idle as dark silicon.

4. Why multicore?

One faster core

  • Needs a higher f, and usually a higher V.
  • Power rises much faster than speed.
  • Speeds up every program with no rewriting.
  • Now blocked by the power wall.

Several slower cores

  • Each core runs at a comfortable V and f.
  • Total power grows roughly with the number of cores.
  • Only helps work that can be split into parallel parts.
  • The programmer must write parallel code (see data races).

SMP: symmetric shared-memory multicore

5. How much faster can more cores make a program?

Recall Amdahl's law. If a fraction p of the work can run in parallel on N cores:

Speedup = 1 ÷ ( (1 − p) + p ÷ N )The serial part (1 − p) does not get faster, so it sets a ceiling: the speedup can never exceed 1 ÷ (1 − p).
Gustafson's law: the hopeful view

Amdahl keeps the problem size fixed. In practice, people give a bigger machine a bigger problem (more pixels, more data). Then the parallel part grows while the serial part stays small. Gustafson's scaled speedup = (1 − p) + p × N. With p = 0.95 and N = 8: 0.05 + 7.6 = 7.65×.

6. Lab 6A: dual-core offload

The board is most likely an ESP32 (a small chip with two cores) running FreeRTOS, a tiny operating system that runs several tasks (small independent programs) at once. The lab compares two setups:

  • Single-core: one core both reads the DHT11 temperature/humidity sensor and computes the sliding mean, max and min.
  • Split: Core 0 only handles the sensor. Core 1 only does the maths. A queue (a first-in, first-out mailbox) carries readings from one core to the other.

How to compare the two setups

  1. Same inputUse the same sample rate and window size in both runs.
  2. MeasureCount windows processed over a fixed time, e.g. 60 s.
  3. RepeatDo 3–5 runs and take the average.
  4. CompareSpeedup = split throughput ÷ single-core throughput.

Expect less than 2×. The queue adds overhead, and the sensor side may be idle much of the time (Amdahl again).

Common mistakes & exam traps

Slips that cost marks
  • Writing P = C·V·f. The voltage is squared: P = α·C·V²·f.
  • Saying "Moore's law ended in 2005". It was Dennard scaling that broke. Transistor counts kept growing.
  • Blaming the power wall on the transistors being too big. The real cause is that voltage could not keep falling because of leakage.
  • Assuming N cores give N× speed. Amdahl's law caps it at 1 ÷ (1 − p).
  • Forgetting that a 15% cut in both V and f gives 0.85³ (not 0.85²) of the power.
  • Mixing up Amdahl (fixed problem size) and Gustafson (problem grows with N).

Practice

V drops by 20% and f stays the same. What happens to dynamic power?

P' = 0.8² × P = 0.64 P, a 36% saving.

V and f both drop by 15%. Find the new power. Then find the power of two such cores.

One core: 0.85³ = 0.614 P. Two cores: 2 × 0.614 = 1.23 P, for up to 1.7× the work if the job splits perfectly.

Why did clock frequency stop rising around 2005?

Dennard scaling broke. Leakage current stopped voltage from falling any further. Without lower V, a higher f raised power density past about 100 W/cm², more than cheap cooling can remove. That limit is the power wall.

Under Dennard scaling (shrink 0.7×), show that power density stays constant.

Power per transistor = C × V² × f = 0.7 × 0.49 × (1/0.7) = 0.49. Area per transistor = 0.7² = 0.49. Density = 0.49 ÷ 0.49 = 1 (unchanged).

p = 0.9 and N = 8. What does Amdahl's law give? What is the ceiling?

1 ÷ (0.1 + 0.9/8) = 1 ÷ 0.2125 = 4.71×. Ceiling = 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10×.

What is "dark silicon"?

Transistors that must stay switched off at any moment because the chip's power/heat budget can't run them all. It is one reason chips now include specialised blocks (GPU, NPU) that are only switched on when needed.

In Lab 6A, why pin the sensor and compute tasks to different cores?

So the compute work never delays the sensor's time-critical handling, and both run truly at the same time. Throughput can rise, but by less than 2×, because of queue overhead and the serial parts.
